## The core idea

A **Money Model is a deliberate sequence of offers** — how you acquire a
customer, what you sell them next, what you sell to a "no", and what keeps
billing — arranged so that if you offer the right thing *at the moment the
customer feels the problem*, you can make as many offers as you like. (The
rental-car counter: $19/day reservation → $100/day paid, five problems
solved.) The thing you sell most is often not the thing you profit most on
(McDonald's makes it on fries and soda). A bad money model kills a business:
it costs more to get a customer than they're worth, so advertising gets cut,
then the business starves.

Compounding: make each customer 2× as valuable, get 2× as many, and get the
cash 2× faster → the business grows **8×** faster. Triple each → **27×**.

## The 30-day rule (Client-Financed Acquisition)

Cover the **full cost to acquire + deliver** a customer within their first
**30 days** — ideally cover it ~2× over. Why 30 days: any business can get
30-day interest-free money on a credit card; if you clear the balance before
month end it behaves like cash, so you can use credit to get a customer, get
paid back inside 30 days, and recycle it for the next. Do that and money
stops being the constraint on growth.

CFA is sharper than LTGP:CAC (from *Leads*). LTGP:CAC asks "is this
profitable eventually"; CFA asks "is this profitable *this month*". A business
can pass the first and fail the second and stall for lack of cash. The lever
isn't only price — it's the **timing** of cash: pull revenue forward with
upsells at point of sale, prepay offers, and larger upfront offers.

## The four offer types, three stages

| Type | Job |
| --- | --- |
| **Attraction** | turn strangers into customers, get cash up front |
| **Upsell** | get people to spend more, now (biggest profit lever) |
| **Downsell** | turn a "no" into a "yes" |
| **Continuity** | keep them paying, month after month |

Sequenced as three stages: **Get Cash** (attraction offer at least breaks
even on acquisition) → **Get More Cash** (add upsells + downsells to maximise
30-day gross profit; this is what makes acquisition *self-financing*, not just
break-even) → **Get The Most Cash** (add/strengthen continuity). Build in that
order. A great subscription you can't afford to sell customers into is not a
business — continuity comes **last**, because on its own it's cash-starved
today (40 buyers at $50/mo = $2,000 now vs. 10 buyers at $1,000 = $10,000 now,
even though the continuity totals more over 20 months).

House rules from the book: every business has a money model; some models fit
some businesses better (design your own, don't copy); **if a customer wants a
refund, give it** ("don't take blood money"); hard selling is for weak
products — make the offer available when the problem is felt and move on if
they pass; be transparent, don't lie (you can't bankrupt a bad reputation).

## Attraction offers (5)

"Free" / "discount" / "$1" are one continuum — anywhere you read one, the
others can substitute. Greater discount = greater perceived deal = more
demand; the greatest is free. You make money on free stuff by making the
*second* free thing conditional on a purchase.

1. **Win Your Money Back** — customer pays now; gets it back (cash or store
   credit) if within Y time and Z rules they **get the result**, **do the
   actions**, or **both**. Actions/results must be **easy to track** (bonus if
   the phone/app already tracks them), **get customers results**, and
   **advertise the business** (post progress, tag, refer, review). Best for
   things people start-and-quit (fitness, skills, business-building). ~10% of
   customers claim it (data across thousands of gyms). The profit is **not**
   from people who fail — it's from people who succeed *and have something
   else to buy*, so always have an upsell ready to apply the winnings to.
   Apply store credit **over a long period** (e.g. $600 → $50/mo off for 12
   months), not up front — people who pay nothing fall off. Only run this if
   your refund rate is already < 5%. Every required meeting is an upsell
   opportunity. "Make everyone a winner" privately, mid-program, to lower
   their failure anxiety and keep them longer. This is a Money Model cousin of
   the Offers-book conditional guarantee.
2. **Giveaways** — advertise a big grand prize (with a stated $ value as
   anchor) for contact info + qualifying actions; pick one winner; then offer
   **everyone else** the same thing at a "partial scholarship" discount off
   the anchored value. Generates many leads pre-qualified on your most
   expensive product. Heavily regulated (sweepstakes law) — check counsel.
3. **Decoy Offer** — a deliberately worse-value option that makes the target
   option obviously right.
4. **Buy X Get Y Free** — "Buy 1 shirt for $20, get 2 free" outperforms "33%
   off / 3 for $20" because more people know the value of *free* than the
   value of one shirt. Always give more free things than paid; cheaper free
   things (3 socks) can beat one expensive free thing (1 shirt). Lengthens
   how long customers stay (Buy 2 Get 2 Free → 4 months) = more upsell
   windows. Great for fast cash from existing recurring customers (cap at 10%
   so recurring cash flow survives). Budget to actually deliver the whole
   prepaid term.
5. **Pay Less Now or Pay More Later** — customer chooses: **pay later** ($0 /
   card on file, billed full price after Y unless they cancel — but only if
   they *qualify* by meeting conditions, e.g. attended) **or pay now** (20–50%
   off + bonuses, no guarantee). "Pay later" lets you advertise "free" and
   gets the card on file; if they take it you follow up with the "pay now"
   offer. Promise a clear yes/no, measurable result inside the window. If
   > 10% of "pay later" people cancel: you over-promised, conditions too
   loose, or price too high.

Also: **Free Goodwill** (the "please review this book" ask) — an attention/
reciprocity play, not really a pricing move.

## Upsell offers (4)

An upsell is "whatever you offer next". Every solved problem reveals the next
one — upsell the solution to it. Upsells often make the *majority* of profit
(the burger: $0.25 → $3.00 with fries/meal/supersize). Upsells fail when the
thing is too different / offered before the problem is felt / offered in a way
they don't believe. Offer the **more profitable** upsell first. Sell more
during the **hyper-buying cycle** (the excited window right after someone
commits to something new — weddings, new hobbies, new babies, moving). Faster
access = more perceived value; put it in their hands before they say yes.

1. **Classic Upsell** — "you can't have X without Y" (car without insurance,
   bike without helmet, exercise course without the nutrition course). Solve
   the next problem the moment they become aware of it. "Get them to say no to
   say yes": "you don't want anything else, do you?"
2. **Menu Upsell** — present a menu of add-ons; let them self-select. Nudge
   toward what makes sense for them.
3. **Anchor Upsell** — present a **5–10× premium option first**, expect
   "the gasp", come to the rescue ("do you care about [the premium feature]?"),
   then present the main offer — which now looks like a great deal. Anchored
   customers spend more than planned, and a handful buy the premium (outsized
   profit, few sales). Never treat the anchor as a fake or the customer will
   too. Main and premium share the **primary** features; only **secondary**
   features differ.
4. **Rollover Upsell** — credit some/all of a prior purchase toward a
   **more expensive** next offer. Four uses: re-engage lapsed customers
   ("winback"), rescue your own upset customer instead of refunding, rescue a
   *competitor's* upset customer (roll over what they owe the competitor),
   upsell current customers. Price the next offer **≥ 4× the credit** so a
   full rollover is at most a 25% discount. Add urgency — make it a one-time
   offer. The "winback campaign" (200 personalised videos offering $4,000
   credit, ~20% took it, ~$1.9M ARR from one day) is the marquee example.

## Downsell offers (3) + the rules

Any offer made after a "no" is a downsell. **Never offer the same thing for
less** — that's discounting, it destroys trust (the car-insurance salesman who
went $5,000 → $400 and lost the whole deal). You can offer something
*different* for less. "No" means no *to this offer*, not to everything. Don't
change price in the moment out of fear; plan prices in advance; customers
talk.

1. **Payment Plan Downsell** — change *how they pay*, not what they get or the
   total. "How much can you put down today?" Deposit + spread the rest.
2. **Trial With Penalty** — for recurring products/services. Free trial, but
   card on file + a commitment + defined actions/meetings; a fee (or lump
   fee) if they don't do them. Gets more paying customers than a no-stakes
   free trial because people actually use it and get value. Use the same
   criteria as Win Your Money Back. Still call it a "free trial". Mid-trial
   check-ins are upsell opportunities (likes it → longer/higher tier; hates it
   → take the blame, offer a better fit; didn't use it → offer to waive the
   fee for a meeting). "First month for $1" justifies asking for the card.
3. **Feature Downsell** — lower the price by **changing what they get** (not a
   discount). Remove features **highest-value first**, drop the price, ask
   "how about now?" — people see the value of what was removed once they see
   the price gap, so they often "re-upsell themselves" back to the full offer.
   **Removing the guarantee** is a feature downsell ("keep the money-back
   option and pay more, or drop it and pay less — which?"). Lever axes:
   quantity, quality, DFY→DWY→DIY, response speed, availability, 1-to-1 vs
   1-to-many, live vs recorded, personalisation, guarantee length/coverage/
   terms. Name the packages (aspirational for the top — "Total
   Transformation"; "The Minimum" for the floor, so "nothing more than the
   minimum?" = say-no-to-say-yes). After two downsells with no yes,
   temperature-check ("1–10, how badly do you want this?"); 8+ → switch to
   payment-plan downselling; 7− → "what would a 10 look like?" and recombine.
   Ask "deal?" / "fair enough?" after each. Barter discounts for reviews /
   testimonials / referrals. Feature-downsold customers who like their custom
   package are Hormozi's **second-highest-value** cohort — do it to a current
   customer before they cancel.

## Continuity offers (3)

Ongoing value for ongoing payment. Sell once, get paid again and again.
Always **last** in the model; always paired with a **prepay upsell** (offer
one-month buyers a discount to prepay 6/12 months — boosts 30-day cash and
stacks recurring revenue). Get people to *start* with bonuses + discounts +
urgency; get them to *keep going* by making sure they use what they pay for.

1. **Continuity Bonus** — a valuable bonus (worth more than the first
   payment) for signing up today; member-exclusive perks for staying.
2. **Continuity Discount** — lower the ongoing cost (free continuum again).
3. **Waived Fee** — waive a setup/join fee for committing.

"No successful continuity business has a standalone membership offer" — they
all attract with trials/challenges then upsell features and prepay.

## Building the model

Per chapter: a doodle, the origin story, how it works, cross-industry
examples, tactical notes, a summary. Any offer works alone; the $100M model
is the **right sequence at the right time**. Design your own — start from the
customer's problem sequence and put an offer at each point they feel a
problem.
